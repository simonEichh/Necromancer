2. IDENTITY AND THE APPROVED IMAGE

Name: Orvoss.
Title: The Blackwake.
Internal model key: Orvoss.
Class: Sovereign.

His presence is a proud naval commander who has spent centuries on the bottom of the sea. He is composed, aristocratic, severe, and unmistakably dead. His wealth appears in the cut of his coat and the craftsmanship of his equipment; the sea has ruined those objects without erasing their quality.

The immediately readable features are:

- Broad, battered tricorn with a skull-and-anchor insignia.
- Fully skeletal face, with deep sockets and cold sea-green eyes.
- Tall, flared officer's collar framing the skull.
- Layered dark naval greatcoat with antique brass trim and epaulettes.
- Open chest revealing a skeletal ribcage and green soul fire.
- Diagonal baldric, waist belt, nautical compass, and restrained chains.
- Very long, torn coat tails with dark sea-green lining.
- Forearm armour, exposed skeletal fingers, tall buckled boots.
- A curved cutlass in his RIGHT hand, lowered outward.
- An antique heavy flintlock pistol in his LEFT hand, lowered.
- Barnacles, a few hanging seaweed strands, and corrosion concentrated in readable patches.

Treat the large front view as authoritative for the resting pose and handedness. Interpret small inconsistencies between generated views sensibly and keep the final model internally consistent. Do not mirror the character accidentally: with front = -Z, his anatomical right is +X, regardless of where it falls on screen.

Do not add a beard, fleshy face, eyepatch covering the defining eyes, oversized comic hook, peg leg, parrot, ship's wheel backpack, modern gun, sci-fi hologram, or giant shoulder cannon. The approved image already supplies enough personality.

3. SILHOUETTE AND PROPORTIONS BEFORE MICRODETAIL

Start with a strong blockout and inspect it from front, three-quarter, side, and rear before spending the full detail budget. Continue refining within this authorized model scope; routine internal stages do not need a new permission question.

Use approximately 7.5-8.5 studs from boot sole to the highest hat point as a starting size, adjusting to match the image and compare sensibly with the existing Sovereigns. Treat these as art proportions, not a new gameplay size or balance decision. Measure the finished rig rather than copying another unit's height attributes.

Keep him tall and lean. The coat shoulders and hat provide breadth; the waist narrows; the coat tails flare back and outward. He has a narrow ribcage and long limbs, not a barrel-shaped armoured torso. Hands can be slightly enlarged so their skeletal construction and weapon grips remain legible.

The hat is broad enough to read instantly, but should not force every portrait to zoom so far out that the skull disappears. The cutlass extends down and out with clear negative space from the leg. Keep its tip above the ground in the rest pose. The pistol's muzzle, stock, and grip must be readable outside the coat silhouette.

The coat must have thickness and a designed volume. Its tails can suggest underwater drift through their rest shape, but should not form a rigid circular cone or huge horizontal fan. Leave believable room for knees, elbows, wrists, and weapon movement.

Keep all equipment within an intentional bounding box. Invisible handles, attachments, or debug geometry must not sit dozens of studs away and ruin portrait framing.

5. THE SKULL AND FACE

Give the skull real structure in three dimensions:

- A shaped cranial mass with a forehead, temporal sides, and rear volume.
- Dark recessed sockets with brow overhangs and separate angular cheekbones.
- A tapered nasal cavity, not a round nose or two painted dots.
- A maxilla and a separate lower jaw with visible negative space.
- A readable row of teeth, with a few grouped pieces where sensible; avoid a smooth white smiling rectangle.
- A chin, mandibular corners, and a narrow skeletal neck with a few vertebral forms.

Build cavities by arranging pale bone around dark inset geometry. The head must remain recognizably skeletal with the eye glow off. Avoid an ivory sphere with two bright dots, a friendly grin, or a fleshy face painted white.

The eyes sit back in the sockets. Use small, saturated sea-green luminous shapes with a controlled brighter centre rather than two enormous Neon balls spilling out of the head. Brow shape and socket depth should give him a severe expression without animated cartoon eyebrows.

The jaw should have its own Motor6D at an anatomical hinge. It can remain still for this pass, but the later finisher line must not require rebuilding his head. Teeth on the lower jaw belong to the jaw assembly. Teeth on the upper jaw belong to the skull.

Use weathered ivory with a darker bone shade beneath cheekbones and around recesses. Keep the face brighter than the coat so it reads under actual game lighting. Avoid using excessive white Neon to simulate bone.

6. THE TRICORN: HIS MOST IMPORTANT DISTANT READ

Construct a convincing three-cornered hat with a visible crown, raised and folded brim, asymmetrical battered tips, and thickness. A flat triangular plate balanced on the skull is insufficient.

Use a small number of substantial wedge/plate groups to establish the crown and brim, then a restrained segmented brass edge. Break or interrupt the trim where the hat is damaged. One corner can droop or split slightly more than the others, as in the image.

The front skull-and-anchor insignia should read as a deliberate badge: an anchor shank, crossbar, curved/fluked ends, and a small skull. It should remain recognizable without requiring a texture or external image asset. Keep it flatter and smaller than the main face.

Add the hatband, a few small rings or chain links, and a clustered barnacle patch near one upper side. Seaweed can hang from a damaged edge. Protect the clean outer tricorn silhouette; a mass of uniform spikes or growth on every edge would obscure it.

Mount the entire hat on its own Hat Motor6D from the skull. Its rest pose is seated naturally on the head, not hovering. Put all hat trim and attached growth under that assembly so the future hat catch can move the complete object. This stage only needs a clean detachable assembly; do not implement the entire finisher or an elaborate runtime reparenting system.

7. RIBCAGE AND THE DROWNED SOUL

The coat opening must reveal an actual skeletal chest with a sternum, paired ribs, dark internal space, and a suggestion of spine behind it. Use curved-looking ribs made from economical angled segments, thick enough to read and separated by visible gaps.

Keep the ribs narrow and anatomically suggestive. Do not replace the chest with a green crystal glued over a solid breastplate. The viewer should see ghost light through the bones.

Build a compact, irregular green soul core behind the sternum and a few subordinate luminous slivers deeper in the chest. The geometry should suggest an internal flame or captive soul without making the whole torso transparent. If a subtle emitter is used, the chest must still work with that emitter disabled and inside a ViewportFrame.

Dark inner carriers must conceal holes in the construction when seen from the side while preserving intended rib gaps from the front. Keep shoulder and waist motion from exposing a giant featureless block behind the open chest.

8. GREATCOAT, COLLAR, LAPELS, AND BELTS

The coat is a ruined officer's garment with a clear tailoring hierarchy:

- A high flared collar behind and beside the skull, with a dark lining and a narrow edge treatment.
- Broad lapels that turn outward and reveal thickness instead of sitting as coplanar rectangles.
- A shaped upper back and shoulder yoke visible in the rear view.
- Front opening framing the chest; substantial brass fastening tabs on one side and a coherent corresponding structure on the other.
- A diagonal leather baldric with a buckle crossing the exposed chest without hiding the soul core completely.
- A waist belt that actually wraps around the body, with a buckle, loops, and holster attachment points.
- A few torn sleeve edges, overlapping cuffs, and controlled material changes.

Use broad overlapping panels, shallow wedges, folded edge strips, and partial trim. Vary panel values enough that black-on-black layers remain visible. Make cloth tears through silhouette changes and deliberate gaps; random bright shards sprinkled over a slab will not read as torn fabric.

Put torso details on the torso, belt details on the pelvis/belt assembly, and sleeve details on the corresponding arm segment. Do not weld a decorative cross-body strap to both independently moving body sections. If a strap crosses the waist, split it visually at a buckle or overlap so the rig can bend naturally.

Keep brass old and restrained. There should be warm metal at buttons, trim, buckles, guard, and badge, with small greener corrosion patches. A bright gold outline around every coat panel would flatten the material hierarchy.

9. THE LONG COAT TAILS AND UNDERWATER DRIFT

The lower coat is a major part of the 700-part investment. Study the rear view: layered hanging tails, overlapping lengths, torn ends, and sea-green interior surfaces.

Use around 8-12 major tail panels distributed around the hips, with the front opening clear. Give the most visible longer panels a second articulated lower segment. Small intermediate strips can be welded detail rather than separate physics objects. Aim for roughly 16-24 cloth joints overall if that produces the best result; the precise count is less important than a coherent rig and affordable secondary motion.

Each panel needs a physical role: outer cloth mass, lining where exposed, one or two fold forms, selected edge trim, and a jagged hem. Avoid coplanar front/back layers that flicker. Shape the tails with a little sweep and taper so they feel waterlogged and suspended, rather than like identical straight rectangular boards.

Use asymmetry in damage, lengths, and growth. Keep the garment's overall balance and the recognizability of an officer's coat.

The tail rig must support gentle lag during turns, a wider spread during future ascent, and a gradual settle. Adjacent panels should not all swing as one synchronized fan. In the finished rest pose, they should not intersect the calves, boot soles, pistol, or cutlass. Test the actual limb/cloth sweep rather than judging only the builder's default camera.

10. EPAULETTES, ARMS, BRACERS, AND SKELETAL HANDS

Build substantial officer epaulettes with a shaped backing plate, rim, and a small grouped fringe. Brass fingers/fringe can be short repeated rods or tapered shapes; keep them clustered under the shoulder edge instead of making giant spiky pauldrons.

Shoulder pieces must follow the correct arm or torso section, with sufficient clearance for the pistol aim and the raised-cutlass order. They should not pass through the high collar when the arm lifts.

Sleeves are fitted at the upper arms, worn at the elbows, and interrupted by blackened metal bracers. Give each bracer an outer plate, smaller overlapping sections, bands or straps, and a few legible rivets. Preserve the distinction between cloth and metal.

Hands are visibly skeletal: palm/metacarpal structure, four fingers and a thumb, separated knuckle shapes, and a believable curved grip. Keep detail concentrated on the visible knuckles and finger segments. You do not need a Motor6D for every phalanx, but a rigid anonymous mitten is insufficient for this unit.

Model fingers curled around the cutlass handle and pistol grip, with the thumb opposing the fingers. The weapon must contact the hand correctly in side view as well as front view. Keep fingertips out of the trigger guard/barrel and preserve a visible trigger area. Future hand pose changes can be supported by one economical grip/finger control if useful; do not add dozens of idle per-finger animations merely to inflate the rig.

11. LEGS AND BOOTS

The lower body should feel narrow and load-bearing. Use shaped thigh and shin carriers under worn trousers, dark cloth between boot and coat, and separated knees that can bend.

Boots need a cuff, layered shaft, several substantial straps/buckles, a shaped instep, a forward toe, a heel, and a visible sole. Use shallow layered geometry to imply worn leather. A long rectangular block with three gold squares on it is not a finished boot.

Keep the soles on the intended floor plane in the static reference stance. The coat may drift but his neutral model stands with weight. Any later finisher ascent is a performance state, not a permanent enormous hover offset. Add a small barnacle patch to one boot as in the art, without covering every surface.

Use ankle joints if they materially help the later ascent and grounding. Set the initial pivots sensibly and keep the heel/toe geometry with its foot assembly.

12. THE CUTLASS

This is the RIGHT-hand weapon and the future finisher command prop. Build it as a separate complete assembly on a CutlassRoot, with a Cutlass Motor6D from RHand.

Give it:

- A substantial curved blade built from a few deliberately aligned segments and wedges.
- A clear spine, thinner cutting edge, taper, and an intentionally shaped tip.
- A darker blade body with a thin restrained sea-green supernatural edge.
- A tarnished brass basket/knuckle guard with enough open space to see the hand.
- A textured-looking grip through a few diagonal wraps or bands, a pommel, and the guard-to-grip connection.
- A little corrosion or age, placed coherently rather than random black/green noise.

The edge should remain continuous when viewed close up. Avoid gaps between blade segments, overlapping transparent glow slabs, a sword as thick as his forearm, or a blade bent like a serrated stairway.

Add attachments BladeBase, BladeTip, and CommandPoint, named and oriented consistently. They provide future trail and gesture hooks, but their existence does not mean trails should always be on. Keep the weapon at a lowered outward diagonal in the reference rest pose, clear of the leg and floor.

13. THE FLINTLOCK

This is the LEFT-hand weapon. Build it as its own assembly on PistolRoot, with a Pistol Motor6D from LHand. It should be an old, heavy, prestigious naval pistol, visibly distinct from Veyr's arcane rifle or a modern handgun.

Include a shaped wood/dark stock, curved grip, butt cap, lock plate, cock/hammer, flint suggestion, frizzen/pan silhouette, trigger guard, barrel bands, and a thick barrel with a properly recessed dark muzzle. Use an economical segmented ring or surrounding metal plus a black inset for the muzzle; an exposed solid cylinder end is not a convincing bore.

Antique brass and worn iron should read independently. The barrel can be a little oversized to suit the proposed supernatural cannon shot, but remains a pistol, not a shoulder cannon permanently attached to his arm.

Use an independent PistolHammer joint if the small moving mechanism is worth animating later. Put Muzzle and Grip attachments in useful local frames. The muzzle's forward direction must be documented; if the project's effects assume -Z, conform to that rather than silently choosing another axis.

At rest the pistol points safely down and slightly away from the body, matching the image. It must be possible to raise it to a firing pose without the hammer clipping the collar or the muzzle passing through the opposite arm. Add an unobtrusive left-side holster/socket for future stowing before the compass gesture. Do not build a duplicate hidden pistol solely to fake that future operation.

The earlier brainstorm called his basic attack Dead Reckoning, but Veyr already uses that name in the current repository. Do not add a duplicate ability name to shared data; this model stage does not decide the final basic-attack name.

14. COMPASS, CHAINS, BARNACLES, AND SEAWEED

The compass is a small hero prop at the waist. Give it a brass housing, dark face, a simple cardinal/needle construction that reads at close range, and a hinged lid. Put it on a CompassRoot connected to the belt/pelvis, with a separate CompassLid joint and a documented left-hand grip/socket attachment for later transfer.

This does not need runtime hand transfer in the current pass. It does need to be a coherent object, rather than a decal-like disc trapped inside the belt. Budget a real lid so the future finisher's closing-compass beat is possible.

Use a few meaningful chain runs: a waist/compass chain, a short hat chain, perhaps a damaged loop beside the hip. Model readable links in visible places, with simplified links or bars where tiny. Use curved segment placement with slight irregularity, not a long perfectly straight ladder. Weld each run to its intended carrier or use a small number of controlled secondary joints; do not introduce physical dangling constraints across the whole character.

Barnacles should look like marine growth: a few short tapered or faceted pale shells with darker recessed centres, differing sizes, growing in patches on plausible exposed surfaces. They should not become white polka dots or a uniform necklace of identical spheres. Group them on hat, one shoulder, and a boot, with only a little growth elsewhere.

Seaweed should be sparse, flat, torn, and desaturated. Use a few thin strips from selected hat/shoulder/hem points, welded or given economical joints if they improve the preview. Keep the skull, chest, and hand silhouettes clear. The model must remain a captain wearing a coat, not a walking bush of kelp.

15. MATERIALS, PALETTE, AND REAL LIGHTING

Match the approved art through a controlled hierarchy. Suggested starting colours, to tune in the actual scene:

- Main coat: charcoal around RGB 20, 25, 26.
- Raised cloth panels: RGB 34, 42, 42.
- Deep recesses: RGB 10, 15, 17.
- Inner lining: desaturated dark sea green around RGB 25, 65, 59.
- Bone: aged ivory around RGB 184, 179, 151, with darker recessed bone.
- Brass: weathered ochre around RGB 137, 111, 64, with selective lighter edges.
- Blackened iron: dark grey around RGB 42, 48, 48.
- Verdigris: muted green around RGB 50, 90, 77.
- Supernatural sea green: saturated mid-value green/cyan, with a small pale hot core.

These are starting values, not approved exact swatches. The actual scene's lighting and bloom decide the final tuning. Separate dark surfaces by value and geometry. Do not let the coat collapse into a featureless black silhouette, or compensate by making all brass white and all dark panels light grey.

Prefer the project's existing Part / WedgePart / Cylinder / Ball approach, using SmoothPlastic, Fabric, and restrained Metal where appropriate. Material choice should support readable forms. Use modest reflectance; inventory portraits remove reflectance, so geometry and colour must carry their own contrast.

Most surfaces stay opaque. Neon is concentrated in eye sockets, the chest interior, and a thin sword edge. Avoid transparent full-body duplicates in the base form. His base form already has ghostly green energy; the separate level-10 awakened form will later use the game's violet shadow vocabulary while retaining his sea-green identity accent.

16. RIG, HIERARCHY, AND ENGINE CONTRACTS

Follow the project's procedural rig conventions exactly where they exist:

- Front = local -Z; anatomical right = local +X; up = +Y.
- HumanoidRootPart is the model's PrimaryPart.
- The design floor is y = 0; root height is measured from that floor to the root's actual rest position.
- Motor6D rest relations must reconstruct the built pose at identity Transform. Bake the intended stance into geometry/C0/C1 consistently; do not require animation code to rescue a broken rest pose.
- Use unique joint names: the animator indexes joints by name, so duplicated generic names can silently overwrite entries.
- Standard joints: Root, Waist, Neck, LShoulder, RShoulder, LElbow, RElbow, LWrist, RWrist, LHip, RHip, LKnee, RKnee, and optional LAnkle/RAnkle.
- Additional joints: Jaw, Hat, Cutlass, Pistol, optional PistolHammer, Compass, CompassLid, and systematically named coat/seaweed controls.
- Every decorative group has one clear carrier. A piece should not have competing welds to both sides of a Motor6D.
- Root and limb carriers are compact, invisible where appropriate, and included in the actual part count.

Suggested skeleton layout, adapting names to existing code as needed:

HumanoidRootPart -> Root -> Hips/Pelvis -> Waist -> Torso
Torso -> Neck -> Skull -> Jaw and Hat
Torso -> each Shoulder -> UpperArm -> Elbow -> Forearm -> Wrist -> Hand
RHand -> Cutlass -> CutlassRoot and all blade/guard details
LHand -> Pistol -> PistolRoot and all pistol details
Pelvis -> each Hip -> Thigh -> Knee -> Shin -> Ankle -> Foot
Pelvis -> Compass -> CompassRoot -> CompassLid
Pelvis/appropriate cloth carrier -> indexed coat-panel joint chains

Set Rig = "Procedural", BuiltBy = "build_orvoss", and accurately measured RootHeight / Height attributes. If you add Pose = "Admiral", implement the minimal corresponding handler or explicitly leave the preview untagged and static; an invented string is not a working animation system. Do not set AttackStyle to an existing unrelated style merely to make it move.

Keep proposed future AttackStyle names out of active runtime dispatch until implemented. In the model-only pass, the correct result can have no AttackStyle at all. StunCore/StunSize may be supplied if measured and useful, but do not copy a rifle-oriented bounding box from Veyr.

Runtime model parts must be noncolliding, massless where expected, unanchored, and connected through the correct welds and motors. Use CanTouch=false and CanQuery=false for cosmetic geometry. For a static staged preview, anchor only its root so the rig can still be inspected; do not bake all limbs permanently anchored into the source model. Existing publish_unit_models.lua anchors published portrait copies, and UnitPortrait unanchors animated copies; respect those distinct use cases.

Do not add a Humanoid, per-limb BodyMovers, individual collision hulls, or a script inside the model. Do not weld all limbs directly to the root. The production Army already supplies the movement block and attaches the root; never create a competing movement system for Orvoss.
